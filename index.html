Boat Research & Development Engineering Intern

Role: Lead Engineering Intern | Summer 2026

Problem: Common Fibers was interested in expanding into the outdoor vehicle market with an affordable composite material vehicle. The two challenges I worked on, with an aim to reduce manufacturing costs, included understanding UV impact on composite materials and reducing their lengthy cure times following lay-up.

Approach: To understand the effect of UV on composites, I manufacturing a matrix of 15 composite material panels that encompassed the variations of lay-up processes and finish sprays used by Common Fibers. I hand-layed and mixed dry fiber, prepreg, and fiberglass laminates, as well as 3 types of resin and resin colorants. To reduce manufacturing time, I laid-up a prototype resistively heated fiberglass tool/mold. This involved using SolidWorks CAM to machine a 4ft x 1ft foam plug on a 3-axis CNC. Once the mold was constructed, with wiring integrated within the fiberglass layers, it was post-cured prior to lay-up.

Result: The manufactured panels were sent to a separate lab for UV testing. The testing will be followed up with mechanical testing. At the end of my internship, I had established the benefit of a bending/flexural test on the samples. The mold was post-cured at 140F while on the plug - and again at 200F off the plug - in an attempt to lock in its dimensions, thus eliminating shrinkage during a cure cycle. A thermal gun was used to monitor temperature, and a voltage dial was used to alter power to the tool during cure. While the first cure cycle failed, it gave us valuable information on the temperature change between the tool underside and surface. Even with severe damage, a second fiberglass part was built and its dimensions remained accurate to that of the SolidWorks CAD.

Effect of Microstructural Defects on Upcycled Composite Materials

Role: Lead Researcher | August 2025 - June 2026

Problem: My research team and I analyzed the viscoelastic behavior of two types of TBDCs against its virgin CFRP counterpart in temperature sweep testing and isothermal stress relaxation testing. Our goal was to understand the effect of the microstructural defects found in the TBD composites on its viscoelastic behavior.

Approach: All experimental testing was done using a TA Instruments DMA Q800. The testing was all done with a strain of 0.01%. Temperature sweep testing started at 35C and increased to 250C at a 3C/min rate, at a constant frequency of 1.0Hz. Stress relaxation testing was performed for 10 minutes at 130C. 130C was determined based on the glass transition temperature (T_g) found in the temperature sweep testing. A 10 minutes recovery period was also in place for the relaxation tests.

Heat Sink & Heat Exchanger CFD

Problem: Analyze the fluid flow around both a heat sink and a heat exchanger. Understand and document the verification of the simulation.

Approach: Simulations were all done using ANSYS Fluent. The heat exchanger simulated flow through the exchanger, whereas the heat sink simulated flow in the domain around the heat sink. A mesh was created for the heat sink. Due to the heat sink geometry, the mesh was broken into sections to prioritize smaller elements around the heat sink, and larger elements - therefore larger interpolations - further away from the entity. Once simulations were conducted, verification was done using the ANSYS Fluent Report systems.

Result: The heat exchanger temperature contours was confirmed using the ANSYS Fluent Flux and Mass Reports. The Flux Report reported a conservation of energy, as the outlet wattage of -3622.41W summed with a heat loss of 1327.95W matches in the input energy. I further confirmed this with the Mass Flow Rate, which showed the kg/s remains constant through the exchanger. While my heat sink was not as successful, it was gained experience in biasing domain elements towards the target being simulated, in this case I modelled the domain with rough 150,000 elements.
