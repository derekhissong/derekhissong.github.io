Boat Research & Development Engineering Intern

Role: Lead Engineering Intern | Summer 2026

Problem: Common Fibers was interested in expanding into the outdoor vehicle market with an affordable composite material vehicle. The two challenges I worked on, with an aim to reduce manufacturing costs, included understanding UV impact on composite materials and reducing their lengthy cure times following lay-up.

Approach: To understand the effect of UV on composites, I manufacturing a matrix of 15 composite material panels that encompassed the variations of lay-up processes and finish sprays used by Common Fibers. I hand-layed and mixed dry fiber, prepreg, and fiberglass laminates, as well as 3 types of resin and resin colorants. To reduce manufacturing time, I laid-up a prototype resistively heated fiberglass tool/mold. This involved using SolidWorks CAM to machine a 4ft x 1ft foam plug on a 3-axis CNC. Once the mold was constructed, with wiring integrated within the fiberglass layers, it was post-cured prior to lay-up.

Result: The manufactured panels were sent to a separate lab for UV testing. The testing will be followed up with mechanical testing. At the end of my internship, I had established the benefit of a bending/flexural test on the samples. The mold was post-cured at 140F while on the plug - and again at 200F off the plug - in an attempt to lock in its dimensions, thus eliminating shrinkage during a cure cycle. A thermal gun was used to monitor temperature, and a voltage dial was used to alter power to the tool during cure. While the first cure cycle failed, it gave us valuable information on the temperature change between the tool underside and surface. Even with severe damage, a second fiberglass part was built and its dimensions remained accurate to that of the SolidWorks CAD.

Effect of Microstructural Defects on Upcycled Composite Materials

Role: Lead Researcher | August 2025 - June 2026

Problem: My research team and I analyzed the viscoelastic behavior of two types of TBDCs against its virgin CFRP counterpart in temperature sweep testing and isothermal stress relaxation testing. Our goal was to understand the effect of the microstructural defects found in the TBD composites on its viscoelastic behavior.

Approach: All experimental testing was done using a TA Instruments DMA Q800. The testing was all done with a strain of 0.01%. Temperature sweep testing started at 35C and increased to 250C at a 3C/min rate, at a constant frequency of 1.0Hz. Stress relaxation testing was performed for 10 minutes at 130C. 130C was determined based on the glass transition temperature (T_g) found in the temperature sweep testing. A 10 minutes recovery period was also in place for the relaxation tests.

Result: In June 2026, I presented my research at the ASME Structures, Structural Dynamics, and Materials Conference in Long Beach, CA. Note: This project would not have been possible without the leadership and guidance from my advisor, Dr. Paulina Díaz-Montiel, and lab colleague, Katie Howard. ** results section to be updated at later date, post paper publication **

Airfoil CFD Simulation

Problem: Analyze the effect of an airfoil's angle of attack on the velocty and pressure in the domain around it. Understand test results and verify.

Approach: The simulations were done using ANSYS Fluent. The airfoil was created using the Fluent Boolean Extraction (or Subtraction) tool. This allowed me to simulate the effect of the airfoil on its immediate domain. Three angles of attack were recorded, 7deg, 10deg, and 15deg. The 7deg and 15deg angles were done by altering the domain with respect to a horizontally-fixed airfoil. The 10deg simulation was done by altering the airfoil's angle relative to a horizontal domain flow.

Result: The velocity and pressure gradients of the 7deg airfoil showcase the relationship between high pressure-low velocity and low pressure-high velocity - best represented right above and below the stagnation point. The equation D=(C_d)(1/2)(pv^2)(A), where A = projected cross area, was used to verify drag force. Using this equation resulted in negative drag force and coefficients for the 7 and 15deg tests, which led to testing at 10deg with the altered set up mentioned above. The 10deg simulation resulted in positive drag force and coefficient, but the coefficient was much too high. As shown in the report, the C_d was much too high, around 62, whereas it would more realistically be around 0.025 to 0.016 (which an estimated projected area).

Heat Exchanger CFD Simulation

Problem: Analyze how temperature and fluid flow through a heat exchanger. Understand and document the verification of the simulation.

Approach: The simulation was done using ANSYS Fluent. The heat exchanger was imported from McMaster-Carr, using a cold-plate exchanger. Water was the simulated fluid with an inlet temperature around 340K and a thermal conductivity of 5.97 W/(m*K). In this instance, the mesh consisted of the piping of the exchanger and the domain where the fluid flows through it. Meshing was done independently between the two components.

Result: Seen in the report linked below, gradients were captured of both the outer pipe and symmetry plan (middle of the pipe) to analyze the change in temperature distribution through the fluid. Fluent Flux Reports of Heat Transfer Rate and Mass Flow Rate were used as verification methods. Both resulted in a net rate of 0, where the heat transfer in was equalled by the combination of the outlet heat and pipe wall (representing heat loss to the wall). Therefore satisfying the Conversation of Energy. The mass flow saw equal inlet and outlet values (in kg/s), which confirms Conversation of Mass.
